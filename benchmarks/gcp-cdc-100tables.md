# 100 tables, 15 columns, 10M and 100M changes — apitap on 1 core (2026-08-15)

Rig on Google Cloud, Debian 12, apitap **0.35.0 installed from PyPI**,
ClickHouse **25.8.29.51**, PostgreSQL **16.15**. 100 tables of 15 columns
(~600 B/row: bigint PK, varchar, smallint/int/bigint, numeric(12,2),
numeric(6,3), bool, two timestamptz, a ~220 B text payload, nullable notes,
varchar ref, jsonb). The **bulk load is deliberately tiny — 100 rows per
table**; all the volume is in the change stream.

Changes are 60/30/10 insert/update/delete, committed in **10,000-row
transactions** (the recorded ceiling is that CDC memory is bounded by the
largest TRANSACTION, not by the row count), generated by 16 parallel writers.
Update targets march across a distinct id window per batch, so the collapser
cannot fold ten events per key — an earlier version of the generator hit the
same low-id rows every batch and flattered the apply path.

Every round is verified per table, pg vs ClickHouse, on `count(*)`, `sum(id)`
and `sum(qty)`. **A round that does not match on all 100 tables is not reported
as a throughput number.**

## Part 1 — 10M changes per round, databases on their own 16-core boxes

apitap on `n2-highcpu-4`; pg and ClickHouse each on `n2-standard-16`.

| round | applied | wall | changes/s | apitap CPU | peak RSS |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 (serial apply) | 9,100,000 | 73.9 s | 123,169 | 28.2 s = 0.38 cores | 111 MB |
| 2 (serial apply) | 9,100,000 | 98.3 s | 92,590 | 27.8 s = 0.28 cores | 113 MB |
| 3 (serial apply) | 9,100,000 | 139.1 s | 65,426 | 27.3 s = 0.20 cores | 115 MB |
| 4 — `APITAP_CDC_APPLY_LANES=8`, 4 cores | 9,046,000 | 51.1 s | **176,987** | 26.8 s = 0.52 cores | 119 MB |
| 5 — same lanes, **caged to 1 core / 1 GB** | 9,154,000 | 52.1 s | **175,532** | 25.2 s = 0.48 cores | 115 MB |

100/100 tables matched after every round.

**10,536,000 changes per minute on ONE core and 1 GB.** The lever was not the
machine: it was `APITAP_CDC_APPLY_LANES=8`, which applies the group's 100 tables
concurrently instead of serially. Rounds 1-3 decay because the destination
tables grow and each window's delete+insert costs more; lanes hide that latency
behind other tables' work. Round 5 started from a LARGER destination than round
4 (25.65M rows vs ~20M) and still matched it, so the 1-core figure is the
conservative one.

Machine load during rounds 4 and 5, sampled on the database boxes themselves:
**Postgres 9-13% of 16 cores, ClickHouse 17-18% of 16 cores.** Nothing in the
rig was the wall.

## Part 2 — 100M changes per round, both databases sharing one 8-core box

GCP had no capacity for a third VM in any zone tried (eight machine families
refused), so pg and ClickHouse were co-hosted. apitap kept its own VM, caged at
1 core / 1 GB.

| round | applied | wall | changes/s | apitap CPU | peak RSS | WAL the slot held |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | 90,100,000 | 955.9 s | 94,257 | 241.1 s = 0.25 cores | 126 MB | 40 GB |
| 2 | 90,100,000 | 1013.9 s | 88,867 | 243.0 s = 0.24 cores | 124 MB | 53 GB |

100/100 tables matched after both rounds.

**These two numbers measure the RIG, not apitap.** The database box ran at load
31 on 8 cores. apitap used a QUARTER of the single core it was given — less CPU
than in Part 1, while going slower. A tool that is slower while working less is
waiting, not computing.

**Memory does not grow with the backlog.** The slot held **53 GB of WAL** while
apitap's peak RSS was **124 MB** — a backlog 400× the process's memory. This is
the recorded rule (CDC memory is bounded by the largest transaction) confirmed
at a far larger scale than it was originally measured at.

## Part 3 — 100M changes per round, ONE 32-core / 256 GB box

GCP would not place a second VM anywhere tonight — ten zones of 16-core pairs,
then four regions of zone-spread pairs, then eighteen attempts at a 4-core
runner beside an already-placed big machine, all refused for capacity. A single
big machine placed on the first try, every time. So this part runs pg,
ClickHouse and apitap on one `n2-highmem-32` (32 vCPU, 256 GB, 500 GB pd-ssd),
with apitap caged to 1 core / 1 GB — the same cgroup methodology every earlier
apitap receipt uses.

| round | applied | wall | changes/s | apitap CPU | peak RSS | WAL held |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | 90,100,000 | 752.0 s | **119,817** | 410.1 s = 0.55 cores | 169 MB | — |
| 2 | 90,100,000 | 734.2 s | **122,719** | — | — | 56 GB |

100/100 tables matched. **Host CPU during the drain: 12% of 32 cores** — pg,
ClickHouse and apitap together used under four of the thirty-two.

Three things this part settles that the earlier parts could not:

**The decay was the destination, not apitap.** Every constrained rig got slower
round over round (10M: 123K → 92K → 65K; 100M on 8 shared cores: 94K → 89K).
On a box with headroom, round 2 was *faster* than round 1 despite the
destination tables being twice as large. Decay is a symptom of a destination
running out of room, not a property of the engine.

**The 8-core box really was the wall.** Same workload, same cage, same code:
94,257/s with the databases on 8 shared cores, 119,817/s with 32. And apitap's
own CPU went UP, 0.25 → 0.55 cores, when the databases stopped starving it. A
tool that uses more CPU while going faster was previously waiting.

**Nothing in the rig is the wall any more, and apitap still is not saturated.**
Host 88% idle, apitap at 0.55 of the one core it was given, 169 MB of a 1 GB
cap, 56 GB of WAL held without moving the RSS. What remains is the shape of the
work itself — a serial WAL decode and the request/response rhythm against
ClickHouse — which is what the apply-lane probe below exists to test.

## Part 4 — three separate VMs, 8-core databases, apitap caged to 1 core / 1 GB

The headline rig. `apitap-cdc-pg` and `apitap-cdc-ch` on their own
`n2-standard-8`; apitap on `n2-highcpu-4` but **cgroup-capped to 1 core /
1 GB**, so it is comparable with every other number in this file. 10M changes
per round, `APITAP_CDC_APPLY_LANES=8`. Both database boxes are sampled during
every drain.

| round | applied | wall | changes/s | apitap CPU | pg busy | ClickHouse busy |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | 9,100,000 | 42.2 s | **215,718** | 25.6 s = 0.61 cores | 26% of 8 | 36% of 8 |
| 2 | 9,100,000 | 42.4 s | **214,682** | 26.8 s = 0.63 cores | 33% of 8 | 41% of 8 |
| 3 | 9,100,000 | 54.4 s | **167,324** | 25.6 s = 0.47 cores | 38% of 8 | 41% of 8 |

100/100 tables matched after each round.

**Peak 12.9 million changes per minute; 10.0M/min by the third round.**

**Apply lanes DAMPEN the decay, they do not remove it.** Rounds 1 and 2 matched
to within 0.5% while the destination doubled — but round 3 fell 22%. (An
earlier draft of this file, written after two rounds, called the curve flat.
Three rounds say otherwise, and the two-round claim was premature.) The
comparison that holds up is against serial apply on a *larger* rig, which fell
47% over the same three rounds:

| | round 1 | round 2 | round 3 | drop |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| serial apply, 16-core databases (Part 1) | 123,169 | 92,590 | 65,426 | −47% |
| 8 apply lanes, 8-core databases (here) | 215,718 | 214,682 | 167,324 | **−22%** |

**And it is still not apitap that slows down.** Its CPU FELL from 0.63 to 0.47
cores as the rate dropped — the signature of waiting, not of running out of
headroom. ClickHouse plateaued at 41% of its eight cores, so it is not CPU
either: what grows is the per-window cost of deleting by key from tables that
keep getting bigger.

**Eight cores per database is not a constraint, and this is the measurement
that settles it.** Going from 16-core boxes (Part 1) to 8-core boxes did not
slow anything down, because the databases use about the same *absolute* CPU
either way — roughly 2 cores for Postgres and 3 for ClickHouse:

| databases | pg | ClickHouse |
|---|---|---|
| 16 cores each | 9-13% → ~1.6-2.0 cores | 17-18% → ~2.7-2.9 cores |
| 8 cores each | 26-33% → ~2.1-2.6 cores | 36-41% → ~2.9-3.3 cores |

The percentage doubles because the denominator halves. The work does not. Both
boxes still finished each round with ~60% idle.

## Part 5 — 100M per round on the Part 4 rig: `changelog=True` vs replica

Same three VMs as Part 4, same cage, same 8 apply lanes — 100M changes per
round instead of 10M, run twice in each mode. Only one variable moves: whether
the apply maintains a replica (delete the window's keys, insert the new rows)
or appends a changelog (insert only; nothing is ever deleted or mutated, and
`<table>__current` derives the replica state as a view).

| mode | round | applied | wall | changes/s | apitap CPU | peak RSS |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| replica | 1 | 90,100,000 | 677.5 s | 132,981 | — | — |
| replica | 2 | 90,100,000 | 684.4 s | 131,643 | — | — |
| **changelog** | 1 | 90,100,000 | 669.5 s | **134,586** | 207.4 s = 0.31 cores | 123 MB |
| **changelog** | 2 | 90,100,000 | 686.7 s | **131,209** | 200.8 s = 0.29 cores | 149 MB |

100/100 tables matched after all four rounds — the changelog rounds verified
through `<table>__current`, not through the raw log.

**Append-only is not faster. It is the same speed.** +1.2% in round 1, −0.3% in
round 2: noise. Both modes also decay by the same ~2.5% from round 1 to round 2.
The delete half of the replica apply was the obvious suspect for the decay seen
in every earlier part, and removing it entirely changed nothing. Whatever sets
this rig's ceiling is not the shape of the apply.

**Choose changelog for what it keeps, not for speed.** After two rounds
ClickHouse held **180,210,100 log rows** — every insert, update and delete with
its LSN and sequence — while the replica destination held only the current
state. That history is the reason to use it.

**And apitap is not the thing that is busy.** 0.29-0.31 of the single core it
was given, 123-149 MB of a 1 GB cap. It spent roughly 70% of both rounds
waiting. A process that is idle two thirds of the time is not the constraint,
which is what Part 6 was built to test.

## Part 6 — query-optimized databases: C3 compute-optimized on local NVMe

The question this part exists to answer: **does buying bigger, faster databases
buy more CDC throughput?** Every earlier part ended with apitap idle and the
databases half-used, which is an argument, not a measurement.

Machine choice came from the quota API rather than from taste: C4 quota is 0 in
this project, so C3 is the top compute-optimized family available; `C3_CPUS` is
24 in every region except us-central1, where it is 200, so a C3 pair only fits
there; and `pd-ssd` is capped at 500 GB per region while local SSD is
effectively unlimited. Hence **`c3-standard-22-lssd`** for both databases — 22
vCPU, 86 GB, and **4 × 375 GB of local NVMe in RAID0** — against the old rig's
`n2-standard-8` with a 200 GB pd-ssd. apitap stayed **caged at 1 core / 1 GB**;
the cage is the identity of this ledger and does not move because the rig got
faster. `changelog=True` throughout, so Part 5 is the like-for-like comparison.

| round | applied | wall | changes/s | apitap CPU | peak RSS | catch-up |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| A — 10M, lanes 8 | 9,100,000 | 41.5 s | **219,054** | 18.6 s = 0.45 cores | 111 MB | 0 rows |
| B — 100M, lanes 8 | 89,100,000 | 560.7 s | **158,898** | 175.1 s = 0.31 cores | 186 MB | 0 rows |
| C — 100M, lanes 16 | 89,100,000 | 484.1 s | **184,042** | 184.6 s = 0.38 cores | 197 MB | 0 rows |

100/100 tables matched after each round, through `<table>__current`.

**Apply lanes are still the lever, and hardware is not.** Round C changed one
environment variable against round B — same rig, same cage, same load, same
100M changes — and gained **+15.8%**. Set against the hardware upgrade that
this whole part was built to test:

| change | gain at 100M per round |
|---|---|
| databases 8 → 22 cores, pd-ssd → local NVMe | +18.1% |
| `APITAP_CDC_APPLY_LANES` 8 → 16 (free) | **+15.8%** |

Doubling a number in the environment bought almost as much as tripling the
hardware. And apitap's own CPU went **up** with it, 0.31 → 0.38 cores: more
lanes did not make it work harder per change, they made it **wait less often**.
That is also why ClickHouse sat at 1-12% — not because it could not keep up,
but because it was never handed enough work at once.

**Bigger databases help only when the load is big.** Against Part 5 and Part 4
on identical apitap and an identical cage:

| load | 8-core databases, pd-ssd | 22-core databases, local NVMe | change |
|---|---|---|---|
| 10M per round | 215,718/s | 219,054/s | **+1.5%** |
| 100M per round | 134,586/s | 158,898/s | **+18.1%** |

Nearly 3× the cores, 2.7× the RAM and ~20× the disk bought **1.5%** at 10M. At
100M it bought 18%, because by then the destination tables hold tens of
millions of rows and the extra ClickHouse capacity finally has something to do.

**The disk was never the wall — and this measurement refutes the hypothesis the
rig was built on.** The prediction was that the old 200 GB pd-ssd, capped near
96 MB/s, throttled a workload writing ~60 GB per round. The sampler says
otherwise: during the drains, **Postgres wrote 0 MB/s and ClickHouse 13-43 MB/s**
to NVMe that can sustain gigabytes per second. The old disk was never more than
half used. Buying 1.5 TB of NVMe bought headroom for something that already had
headroom.

**Nothing in this rig is busy.** Postgres 6-7% of 22 cores, ClickHouse 8-12% of
22 cores, apitap 0.31-0.45 of its single core. What is left is round-trip
latency — each window waits for ClickHouse to answer before the next one starts
— and waiting appears as neither CPU nor MB/s anywhere in the rig.

### The harness bug this rig exposed, and the proof that it was a harness bug

The first attempt reported 219,054/s-class numbers but verified only 92/100:
the tables written LAST were short, in a clean gradient (t92-95 missing 5,000
rows, t98-100 missing 40,000). That is the signature of a drain stopping at a
WAL position, not of corruption. This rig's writer does 10M changes in **10 s**
where the old rig took ~45 s, and with `synchronous_commit=off` Postgres had
not flushed the writer's final commits when the drain asked where the WAL
ended.

That was a hypothesis, so it was tested rather than asserted: a **catch-up
drain with no writer running** picked the missing rows up and the destination
went to **100/100, including `sum(id)` and `sum(qty)`**. No rows were lost —
they were in the WAL, unread. This is correct CDC behaviour: one drain catches
up to *now*, and the remainder belongs to the next drain. The fault was the
harness demanding exact equality immediately after a single bounded drain, an
assumption that held only while the writer was slow.

The harness now (1) flushes and settles between writer and drain, and (2) runs
an **untimed catch-up drain after every timed round and prints how many rows it
had to pick up**. Both clean rounds above report **0 rows**, which is what makes
their timed numbers complete rather than merely plausible.

## Part 7 — one box, and the parallel-slot result that ends the hunt

Every part so far ended the same way: apitap idle, databases idle, nothing
saturated. Two candidates remained — the network between the VMs, and something
serial nobody had looked at. This part tests both on a single
`c3-standard-88-lssd` (88 vCPU, 346 GB, 16 × 375 GB local NVMe in RAID0)
running Postgres, ClickHouse and apitap together over loopback.
`changelog=True`; apitap caged at 1 core / 1 GB as always.

### Co-locating is worse, not better

| round | one box | three VMs (Part 6) | change |
|---|---|---|---|
| 10M, lanes 8 | 122,612/s | 219,054/s | **−44%** |
| 100M, lanes 8 | 121,789/s | 158,898/s | **−23%** |

100/100 verified, catch-up 0 rows, both rounds. **The network was never the
problem.** Sharing a box costs far more than removing the network saves: the
same work cost **5.2 µs of CPU per change here against 2.0 µs on separate
VMs** — 2.6× more CPU for identical work, the signature of cache and
memory-bandwidth contention with ClickHouse.

Note also that the one-box rate is **flat across a 10× load range** (122,612
then 121,789) where the three-VM rig fell 219K → 159K. A rate that ignores load
size is the signature of a fixed serial bottleneck.

### The serial bottleneck, found

During the 100M drain, with 88 cores idle:

```
99.9%  postgres: walsender ... START_REPLICATION      <- pegged
 0.41  apitap (1-core cage, 1 thread)                 <- idle 59%
```

**Postgres decodes each replication slot in ONE walsender process.** That
process saturates a core and cannot use a second one, no matter how large the
machine. This is what every earlier part was actually measuring.

### Splitting the slot: 100 tables across 4 slots

100 tables in 4 groups of 25, each group its own `transfer()` call — slot names
hash their sorted membership (`run.rs:546`), so disjoint groups never collide —
and **each group in its own 1-core / 1 GB cage**.

| group | tables | applied | wall | changes/s |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| g0 | 25 | 22,525,000 | 319.2 s | 70,561 |
| g1 | 25 | 22,525,000 | 322.4 s | 69,876 |
| g2 | 25 | 22,525,000 | 315.6 s | 71,367 |
| g3 | 25 | 22,525,000 | 313.3 s | 71,891 |
| **total** | **100** | **90,100,000** | **323 s** | **278,947** |

100/100 tables verified via `__current`. **16.7 million changes per minute** —
against 121,789/s for one slot on the same box, a **2.29× gain**, and the
largest single improvement in this whole file.

Four walsenders ran at 99.9% each; the four apitap processes used **1.60 cores
in total** of the four they were allowed. Whole-box cost: **~7 cores of 88** for
source, engine and destination together.

### Why 2.29× and not 4×, and what that implies

Every slot decodes the **entire** WAL and discards what is not its own;
publications filter at output, not at decode. Fitting the two measured points to
`wall = D + O/N`:

- N=1: D + O = 731 s
- N=4: D + O/4 = 323 s
- → **decode ≈ 187 s (repeated by every slot), output ≈ 544 s (shared out)**

So ~74% of walsender cost divides and ~26% never does. **Extrapolation, not
measurement** — the model is fitted on two points and ignores WAL read
amplification entirely:

| slots | predicted /s | per minute | % of ceiling | per-slot contribution |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 4 (measured) | 278,947 | 16.7M | 58% | 69,737 |
| 8 | ~353,000 | 21.2M | 73% | 44,100 |
| 16 | ~408,000 | 24.5M | 85% | 25,500 |
| 36 | ~446,000 | 26.7M | 92% | 12,383 |
| 100 | ~468,000 | 28.1M | 97% | 4,683 |
| ∞ | 482,000 | 28.9M | 100% | — |

Three things this says. **Postgres has a hard ceiling as a CDC source at
~29M changes/minute**, and it belongs to Postgres, not to apitap. **The useful
range is N=4 to N=16**; beyond that each slot buys a collapsing share while
costing a full pegged core. And **100 slots is dominated on every axis** — it
needs 100 cores of walsender alone (larger than any machine GCP would place
tonight), and 100 readers of the same ~60 GB WAL turns into terabytes of reads
the moment they drift out of page-cache lockstep, which the model does not
capture and which would make it *slower* than N=4.

The sweep N = 1, 2, 4, 8, 16 has not been run. Until it is, only the N=1 and
N=4 rows above are receipts. (It has now — see Part 8.)

## Part 8 — the slot sweep, on the shipped `slots=N` (v0.36.0 from PyPI)

The direct test of "can one Postgres reach 100M changes/minute": sweep
`slots=N` over the same 100 tables × 100M changes, all on one rig —
`apitap-s-pg` and `apitap-s-ch` both `c3-standard-44-lssd` (44 vCPU, local
NVMe; the 88-core shape was refused for capacity, and C3 is the top
compute-optimized family this project has quota for — C3D/H3/C4 are all 0),
runner `c3-highcpu-22`. This is the RELEASED wheel doing the whole thing in
one process — `apitap.transfer(..., changelog=True, slots=N)` — in a fixed
8-core / 8 GB cage for every point, so the points compare. Every point:
fresh reset, bootstrap, 100M changes, timed drain, untimed catch-up (0 rows
everywhere), verify 100/100.

| slots | applied | wall | changes/s | per minute | gain | apitap CPU | RSS | pegged walsenders |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | 90,100,000 | 505.7 s | 178,166 | 10.7M | — | 0.33 | 174 MB | 1 |
| 4 | 90,100,000 | 230.4 s | 391,031 | 23.5M | +120% | 0.83 | 287 MB | 4 |
| 8 | 90,100,000 | 203.4 s | 443,051 | 26.6M | +13% | 0.94 | 445 MB | 8 |
| 16 | 90,100,000 | 189.2 s | **476,181** | **28.6M** | +7.5% | 1.08 | 667 MB | 15-16 |
| 32 | — | — | — | — | — | — | — | fd limit, below |

**The knee is at N=4–8, and the answer to 100M/minute is NO — not from one
Postgres.** 476,181 changes/s is 3.5× short of the 1,666,667 needed, and the
curve's shape says the rest of the distance is not purchasable with more
slots: doubling 8→16 walsenders bought 7.5%. The Part 7 model (fitted at
~74% shardable) proved optimistic — actuals came in 9% under it at N=8 and
15% under at N=16; the non-shared fraction grows with N.

**apitap's cost stays flat at ~2.1 µs/change across the whole sweep** (0.33 →
1.08 cores while throughput grew 2.7×). The per-slot bill is Postgres's: one
pegged core per walsender, 16 cores of decode at N=16 against apitap's 1.

**N=32 failed honestly: `Too many open files` (errno 24)** during the
parallel bootstrap — 25 concurrent groups (100/⌈100/32⌉) × bulk-load pipes
blew the runner's default 1024-fd ulimit. That is a real operational cost of
large N: file descriptors scale with N × parallel. Raise `LimitNOFILE` (or
cap group concurrency in the engine — open follow-up) before running slots
past ~16. The predicted gain there was ~3-5%, which the knee already made
moot.

**Recommendation this sweep buys:** `slots=4` is the value engine defaults
should eventually pick — it captures 82% of the N=16 rate at a quarter of
the source's CPU bill. `slots=8-16` only for burn-down races where the
source box is disposable. The empirical ceiling of one Postgres source on
the best rig this project can place is **~28.6M changes/minute**; past that,
the architecture answer is multiple sources (shard the database), not more
slots.

### Two harness bugs this part exposed

**`wait` with no arguments deadlocks against `exec > >(tee …)`.** The process
substitution's `tee` is a child of the shell, so a bare `wait` waits for it
forever while the script holds the pipe open. The first parallel run sat idle
17 minutes at that line. Fixed by collecting PIDs and waiting on each.

**Killing a psql client does not stop its server-side work.** `pkill` on the
writer left backends still executing `CALL gen_round(...)`, which uses dynamic
SQL and therefore kept inserting into tables the reset had just re-created *by
name* — the bootstrap snapshot loaded 5.2M rows where 10,000 were expected. The
reset now runs `pg_terminate_backend` on stray writers first and prints the
count it killed.

## What this rig does NOT say

- **100M changes/minute was not reached, and Part 7 says it is not reachable
  from a single Postgres.** The best verified figure is **16.7M/min** (Part 7,
  four parallel slots, 100M per round); single-slot bests are 13.1M/min (Part 6
  round A) and 11.0M/min (Part 6 round C). 100M/min needs 1,666,667 changes/s,
  and the fitted ceiling for *any* number of slots against one Postgres is
  ~482,000/s — a factor of 3.5 short even at infinity.
- **No part of this file ever saturated apitap.** Its CPU ranged from 0.24 to
  0.64 of the single core it was given, across every rig, every load size and
  every mode. Every number here is a property of its rig — and, from Part 7, of
  Postgres's single-threaded logical decoder.
- **Part 4's 215K/s is not directly comparable to Part 1's 175K/s.** Part 1's
  round 5 drained into a destination already holding 25.65M rows; Part 4's
  round 1 started nearly empty. The like-for-like comparison is round 1 against
  round 1: 123,169/s with serial apply versus 215,718/s with eight apply lanes,
  on a smaller rig.
- The applied count is 9.1M / 90.1M rather than 10M / 100M because the
  generator's update windows are not always full. **Every rate is computed from
  what apitap actually applied**, never from the count that was intended — an
  earlier version of the harness divided the intended 10,000,000 by the wall of
  a drain that moved zero rows and printed 4.6M changes/s. That code path was
  removed: a drain reporting zero rows now fails the round instead.
- Three separate VMs on a real network, unlike every earlier apitap receipt,
  which ran the databases on the same host as the engine over loopback. These
  numbers are therefore not directly comparable to the 0.5-core ledger.
- The measured client cost here is **~2.8 µs of CPU per change**
  (25.2 CPU-s / 9.15M). The `ch-ingest-r3` ledger recorded 12.3 µs/change on
  15-column rows; that was older code (before PGO, the round-3 knives, and
  ClickHouse patch-part deletes) on a different CPU. A sizing estimate built on
  the old figure predicted ~2 cores would be needed for 10M/min. The real answer
  is under half of one.
